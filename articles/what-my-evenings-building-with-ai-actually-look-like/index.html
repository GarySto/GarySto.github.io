What My Evenings Building With AI Actually Look Like

7 October 2026 · Updated 12 October 2026 · Gary Stow, performance marketing and AI

Written by Gary Stow with help from AI tools. Every fact is checked and every article is approved by Gary before it goes live.

Most of my personal project work happens in the evenings, and people sometimes picture that as hours of typing code in a dark room. The reality is quite different, so this is what a typical evening actually looks like, including the bits that go wrong.

Quarter to nine

My two daughters are in bed at 8:30, my wife settles in with her TV programmes, and by about 8:45 or 9 o'clock I'm at the PC. From there I work on whichever project needs me: the website, my trading project, my personal brand, or one of the side projects like the village dashboard that shows my AI teams at work.

I keep going until one of three things happens: I run out of Claude tokens, my eyes start dropping and I'm too tired, or the job's done. That's why I'm often still up at 1 or 2 in the morning.

Most evenings are fixing, not building

I'm usually carrying on from where I left off, building the next section of something, or more and more often troubleshooting and improving what's already running. Things break all the time, and two real examples show what that looks like.

The first was command prompt windows: my automations are meant to run silently in the background, but windows kept flashing up on the screen, one after another, like a machine gun of little black boxes. It turned out that background jobs which run without a window were starting other tools, and Windows was giving every one of those its own visible window. The fix was a small piece of code that tells every background job to keep its child processes hidden, plus a regular check that puts it back if anything ever removes it.

The second was my automated trading team, which suddenly stopped making trades altogether. Not every quiet day is a bug, though, because on one of those days the system correctly showed that not a single stock out of 906 scanned had scored high enough to buy, which was a genuine market condition. Telling a quiet day apart from a broken system is now built into a permanent check, a watchdog that flags when a model has gone too many market days without a trade.

Tonight

As I write this, the evening has been spent on my articles. Some recent feedback on Reddit told me some of my shorter posts read like AI, and I've taken that on board, so I've been rebuilding them with far more real detail. Alongside that, I'm working out how to get the site's 30-odd pages ranking on Bing and Google, because I want better performance than I'm getting.

The three AIs I use

I spread the work across three AIs, each for a different job:

  • Wade, my own AI running on my PC, which helps manage my trading and website projects with no token cost
  • Claude, for most of the coding, troubleshooting and building
  • Copilot, for ideas, working out what's possible on my PC, and making images

Splitting the work like that keeps the costs down, because no single tool takes all the strain.

My rules

I work to strict rules: I don't use my job to get ahead on these projects, and I keep everything as low cost as possible, ideally free. I want to show that anyone can do what I do, and that it's a fair, level playing field.

Protecting the work

Small steps with guardrails beat big leaps that break everything at once. I document every checkpoint, keep older files I can fall back on, and check what the AI tells me rather than trusting it blindly. When an evening goes badly, that's what lets me pick it up again the next night instead of starting over.
