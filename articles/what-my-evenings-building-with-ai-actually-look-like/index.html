What My Evenings Building With AI Actually Look Like

7 October 2026 · Gary Stow, performance marketing and AI

Most of my personal project work happens in the evenings and at weekends once my daughters have gone to bed, often until one or two in the morning. People sometimes picture that as hours of writing code, but a typical evening looks quite different.

Carrying On Where I Left Off

Depending on the project, I am usually carrying on a previous chain of work, building the next section of something, or, more and more often lately, troubleshooting, tweaking and looking for ways to improve what is already running. A real example from this week: command prompt windows kept opening on my PC when everything was meant to be running silently in the background. Another time, something was clearly off with my automated trading team because it had stopped making trades altogether, and the evening went on finding out why.

The Three AIs I Use

I use three LLMs, each for a different job. Wade is my local AI, built and running on my own PC, and its job is to help manage my automated trading and my website. Claude is what I use for most things, coding, troubleshooting and building out my projects and ideas. The third is the AI assistant that comes with my Office subscription, which I use a lot for ideas, for working out what is and is not possible with AI on my personal computer, and for image creation, so I am spreading my usage and costs rather than leaning on one tool for everything.

My Rules

I like to work to strict rules. I do not use my job to get ahead on these projects, and I keep everything as low cost as possible, ideally free. I want to show that anyone can do what I do, and that it is a fair, level playing field.

What Usually Goes Wrong

Plenty goes wrong, and that is part of it. I have chased problems that did not exist, because the AI hallucinated or forgot the rules and instructions I had given it. Sometimes the conversation goes round in circles. When that happens I have learned to take a breath, go for a walk, take a different approach, ask the question slightly differently, or just start a brand new chat.

The other thing I have learned is to protect the work. I document every checkpoint, I keep older files I can fall back on, and I check what the AI tells me rather than trusting it blindly. Small steps with guardrails beat big leaps that break everything at once.
