Prompt Engineering for Marketers: The Six Parts I Teach

31 October 2026 · Gary Stow, performance marketing and AI

Written by Gary Stow with help from AI tools. Every fact is checked and every article is approved by Gary before it goes live.

Prompt engineering for marketers is the skill of getting what's in your head into the instruction you give an AI, so the model doesn't have to guess what you meant. I teach it as six parts, in the same order every time, because once the structure becomes habit you stop having the long back and forth that most people assume is just how AI works. It's the structure I've presented to rooms of marketers, and it's the one I still use myself every day.

The six parts of a good prompt

Role. Start by telling the model who it's for this job, for example "you are a senior account strategist". That one line sets the level and the point of view of everything that follows, so it's worth getting right before you type anything else.

Context. This is everything the model can't see, such as the client, the goal, what has already happened and what the numbers are for. It's the part people skip most often, and I'll come back to why in a moment.

Task. Next comes the actual thing you want done, written in one or two plain sentences. If you can't say it that simply, the thinking probably isn't finished yet and the model will end up finishing it for you.

Constraints. Here you say what the model must stick to and what it must avoid. Think of it as the fence around the answer, so that the model has room to work without wandering off somewhere you didn't want it to go.

Format. Tell it how you want the answer back, whether that's a table, bullet points, a short email or a slide outline. Deciding this up front saves you from reshaping the output by hand afterwards.

Length. Finally, say how long the answer should be. It sounds small, but a 100 word summary and a 1,000 word report are very different jobs, and the model can't tell which one you're picturing.

If you want something to copy and keep, this is the skeleton I'd start from. Swap the square brackets for your own details and the rest takes care of itself.

Role. You're a [role] working on [type of job].
Context. [Client, goal, what has already happened, what the numbers are for.]
Task. [What you want done, in one or two plain sentences.]
Constraints. Stick to [what it must use] and avoid [what it must not do].
Format. Give me [table, bullets, short email, slide outline].
Length. Keep it to [number of words, slides or paragraphs].

Using the structure gets you the answer you wanted much quicker, and it saves the back and forth. That back and forth is really the model trying to find the information you didn't give it in the first place.

Why the same data can give you completely different answers

A good way to see this for yourself is to take one spreadsheet you know well and ask about it three ways. Ask for a summary and you get a summary of the numbers, ask what might be causing the changes and you get a discussion of causation and correlation, and ask what to do next and you get a list of things to investigate and where the opportunities might be. Nothing about the data changes between those answers, only the instruction does, and that's the whole argument for taking the prompt seriously.

People also tend to ask the same two things whenever AI comes up, which are how and what's next. They hear big claims about what AI can do and want the steps, the prompt and the method. The six parts are the most useful answer I can give to the first of those, because they turn "use AI for your reporting" into something you can actually type.

The most common mistake is missing context

When I look at prompts that go wrong, the cause is almost always the same, because people don't give the model enough context. We assume it knows what we mean, and it can make a decent attempt without the extra information, but the answer is never as good as it would have been with it.

The fix is to write down the things you'd explain to a new colleague on their first day. That means who the client is, what good looks like for them and what you already know. If you'd have to tell a person, you have to tell the AI.

Two ways a prompt goes wrong

Prompts fail at both ends of the scale, and the two failures look very different. The first is the over-long prompt that micromanages. If you want a 300 word document, say so, let the model do its thing, and correct the style afterwards if it isn't right. If you're going to get to the level of dictating every sentence in advance, you might as well have just written it yourself, because at that point you don't need AI.

The second is the very light prompt. With hardly any context the AI is free to bring in anything, and that's where most of what people call slop or hallucinations comes from. It comes down to the quality of the prompt, and the six parts sit between the two extremes, giving the model enough to point it in the right direction without writing the answer for it.

My own lesson with header images

I learned this the hard way on my own website. My early prompts for article header images weren't specific enough, so the pictures all came out looking much the same and didn't match what I had in mind. I hadn't told the model what I didn't like, why I didn't like it, or that I wanted variety across the set. Once I went back and changed the prompts to say those things, the results changed with them.

Every AI needs a slightly different approach

I treat each AI I use as a different personality, and I adjust the same structure for each one. A local model on my own PC needs very specific, less friendly prompts because it has a smaller memory to work with. My personal Copilot needs me to be more forceful, or it asks a lot of questions before it gives me an answer. Claude can work across many files and projects at once, but it uses up my allowance quickly, so I need to know what I want before I start.

The six parts stay the same for all three. What changes is how much detail goes into each one and how firmly it's worded.

What I'd tell a marketer starting today

Write your next prompt with all six parts, even if it feels like overkill for a small job, and compare the answer with what you'd normally have got. Most people only need to see that difference once, and it's the quickest way to find out which of the six you tend to skip.
