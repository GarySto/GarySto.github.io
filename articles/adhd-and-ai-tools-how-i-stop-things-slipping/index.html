ADHD and AI Tools: How I Stop Things Slipping

6 October 2026 · Updated 12 October 2026 · Gary Stow, performance marketing and AI

Written by Gary Stow with help from AI tools. Every fact is checked and every article is approved by Gary before it goes live.

ADHD looks different for everyone, so I can only speak for my own. For me it's two things: getting distracted, and forgetting. An email goes unnoticed, or I say I'll do something and it vanishes from my head completely. For years that meant a steady stream of small things slipping, and every now and then a big one.

What slipping actually looked like

The worst version was client meetings, where I'd commit to a meeting, get pulled into something else, and the meeting would creep up on me without my noticing. Then I'd suddenly have an hour to put together a PowerPoint presentation or an analysis of an industry that deserved a day. It became a mad rush, over and over, and it wasn't because I didn't care about the work. My brain just didn't hold on to it.

Reminders that come to me

So I stopped relying on my memory and built a system that does the remembering for me. I use Copilot Cowork to send me scheduled emails every day:

  • 8am: an email I walk into at the start of the day, telling me what's coming up, plus a tip or skill I want to keep learning.
  • 12pm: any tasks still outstanding.
  • 4pm: the same again, while there's still time in the day to act on it.

The important part is the order, because the emails put my work in priority order: by when I said I'd deliver something, or by how big the impact is. A client whose accounts have all been switched off goes to the top. So does anything I've promised for Friday. That means I can work through the list methodically and quickly instead of reacting to whatever shouted loudest.

The difference has been real, and I can plan my week properly, and those last-minute rushes have become much rarer.

The trick that gets me started

One small thing works really well for me: getting the system to draft the email I need to send. Seeing a ready-made draft sitting there is a reminder that the task needs finishing, and it removes the blank page, which is very often exactly where I get stuck.

Checks that catch what I miss

When your attention jumps around, it's easy to miss a detail or leave something half done. I've built checks that look back over my work to make sure it's accurate and complete. They don't replace my judgement, but they catch a lot of what slips through.

The tool that didn't work

Not everything I've built has helped, though. I wanted help writing social media content, so I built a tool at home where I could drop in my ideas and get back a skeleton of a post to build out.

The problem was the structure, because I had to go to a specific folder, and write my ideas in a specific style, before the system could do anything with them. And I wasn't always at the PC when the ideas hit me. The system collapsed after its first week because I simply never used it.

It just wasn't convenient, and the tools that stick for me are the ones that come to me, like the reminder emails, rather than ones I have to remember to go to.

The same idea at home

I use the same approach for my personal projects. Wade, the AI that runs on my own PC, does the same job for my evening projects, nudging me about what's due so nothing drifts. Without that, the evening projects would drift the same way work used to.

The human side still matters

The tools help, but they're not the whole answer. I try to take proper breaks through the day so I come back focused enough to finish things. I also run, which does more for my head than any app.

And ADHD is part of why AI clicked for me at all. My first go at learning to code fizzled out because I couldn't hold my focus on it, whereas AI was different because it caught my fascination, and building tools around the way my brain actually works, rather than fighting it, has been one of the most useful things I've done with it.
