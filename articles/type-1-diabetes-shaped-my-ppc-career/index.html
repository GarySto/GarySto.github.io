How type 1 diabetes shaped my PPC career

29 October 2025 · Gary Stow, performance marketing and AI

Written by Gary Stow with help from AI tools. Every fact is checked and every article is approved by Gary before it goes live.

I was about 15 when I was diagnosed with type 1 diabetes, in 1998 or 1999. I can't remember the exact date, but I remember how it happened. We were living in Gibraltar, and my mum, who was working in an old people's home at the time, noticed I was drinking a lot and going to the toilet a lot. She got me checked, and my sugar levels were so high the machine couldn't even give a reading.

I was rushed down to the local doctors, who agreed I needed to go straight into hospital. At 15 I was too old for the children's ward and too young for the adults' ward, so they put me on the adults' ward, surrounded by old Spanish men watching Spanish TV. I was bored and frustrated, and I tried to escape a couple of times. I learned to do my injections by practising on an orange, and that, essentially, was my life from then on.

What came after the orange

I've been back in hospital a few times since. I've had diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA), a few incidents with low blood sugar, and diabetic retinopathy in my eyes, which has meant surgeries. Plenty of people I've worked with have seen me on business calls wearing an eye patch when my eyesight goes, and have seen me before and after the operations. It's who I am, and I'm not ashamed of it.

I spent a lot of the late 90s and early 2000s in hospital, and that's where I found Deadpool, Wade Wilson, an anti-hero who was terminally ill but couldn't die. At the time I felt like neither could I. He also made me laugh, and when you're dealing with a lot, a laugh is worth so much. It's why the local AI that helps run my website and trading today is called Wade.

A lifetime of numbers

I've been living with type 1 diabetes for more than 25 years now. That's a long time to be constantly thinking about numbers. Insulin doses, blood glucose levels, carb counts, stress levels, everything is tracked, tweaked, and monitored. And weirdly enough, that obsession with numbers has ended up being one of my biggest strengths in PPC.

It took me a while to realise it. When you're in the thick of managing a chronic condition, you don't stop to think that it's training you for campaign optimisation. But looking back, it's clear. Diabetes taught me how to analyse, how to react quickly, and how to understand the impact of small adjustments. When I change my insulin dose by a fraction, I feel it. When I tweak a CPC by a few pence, I see it in the data. It's the same muscle, just flexed in different ways.

Of course, it's not all smooth sailing. There are days when I've been up all night dealing with high sugars, and then I'm expected to be sharp in a morning meeting. There are moments when my blood sugar drops mid-presentation and I have to pause, recalibrate, and carry on. These aren't just inconvenient, they're exhausting. But they've also taught me resilience. I know what it feels like to get something wrong and have to fix it fast. That's true in my body, and it's true in my campaigns.

My eyes add their own challenge. Reviewing accounts full of tracking codes and custom parameters, with all those symbols, is genuinely straining, and it's one of the reasons I build tools that take the squinting off me. Even the red lights on my PC at home are there because they're easier on my eyes in the evenings.

Diabetes and ADHD together

And then there's the hyperactivity. Living with both diabetes and ADHD, which I've written about separately in Thriving in Tech with a Neurodiverse Brain, means I can swing between intense focus and total distraction. I can be the most productive person in the room, or the most chaotic. It's a lot. And I know it can be challenging for new managers or teammates to figure me out. But once they do, once they understand how I work, the results speak for themselves.

Because here's the thing: that hyperfocus is a superpower. When I lock onto a campaign, I go deep. I see patterns others miss. I test, I iterate, I optimise like mad. And yes, sometimes I need to be reminded to take a break or eat something. But the work I produce in those moments is some of my best.

What I want others to know

I've been lucky to work with teams who get it. Who don't just tolerate my quirks but embrace them. They've asked questions, made space, and supported me when I needed it. And that's what I want others with similar health backgrounds to know: great teams and companies do exist. You don't have to hide who you are. You don't have to apologise for being different. Your experience, however complex, is valid and it can be a strength.

This isn't a polished, inspirational story. It's just my reality. Diabetes is hard. ADHD is hard. But both have shaped how I think, how I work, and how I lead. They've given me a unique lens on data, performance, and human behaviour. And in PPC, that's gold.

So if you're managing a condition, wondering if it's holding you back, know that it might just be your edge. With the right support, the right team, and the right mindset, you can thrive.
