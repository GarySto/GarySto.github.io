How to Learn AI as a Marketer With No Coding Background

8 October 2026 · Updated 12 October 2026 · Gary Stow, performance marketing and AI

Written by Gary Stow with help from AI tools. Every fact is checked and every article is approved by Gary before it goes live.

I have no computer science degree, no AI exams, no certifications and I'm not an engineer. Everything I've built, from a local AI assistant on my own PC to automation teams that run a demo trading account and help run this website, I've learned by building it. If you're a marketer wondering whether you can do this without a technical background, this is how it went for me, including the bits that didn't work.

The first attempt failed

A few years ago I was looking at a move into an analytics and insights role, and it was pointed out to me that Python was a skill I'd need and a gap I had. So I tried to learn it. I did a couple of courses: one in the Microsoft Learn section, and a free one I think was on Coursera.

I got through "hello world", but after that I got confused, then lost, and with my ADHD I drifted off to something else. Learning for the sake of learning just didn't stick.

What changed was having a reason

Two things changed it, and the first was AI itself. The more I explored AI tools, the more I could see what they were going to mean for productivity in business. It caught my fascination in a way Python never had, and I started building small agents and automations of my own.

The second was a project, because I had an idea about the stock market and wanted my own tool to find the opportunities I should be looking at. That meant learning GitHub and Python properly, because the next part of the project needed it.

Learning with a mentor beside you

The big difference the second time round was working with Claude. Instead of following a course at someone else's pace, I was building something real, and every time I didn't understand a step I could ask why. I could see what I was doing, where it was going and why it worked.

That's the difference between a group course and an individual mentor. A course has content to get through. A mentor stops when you're stuck.

What I can actually do now

I want to be honest about this, because a lot of "learn AI" content isn't. I still don't fully understand Python. Without Claude, I couldn't write a script from scratch.

What I can do is follow instructions properly: go and find this line, change it to that, run it, read the output. One of my biggest early mistakes was indentation. I had no idea how much it matters in Python until things kept breaking because a line sat in the wrong place. I could ask Claude to write every script for me, but I try to get hands-on wherever I can, because that's where the learning sticks.

My evenings

Most of this happens after the kids are asleep, and it often runs well past midnight. I've described a typical night, including what breaks, in what my evenings building with AI actually look like.

The tools keep moving

Something nobody warned me about is how often the ground shifts. Over the last few years I've been through a lot of model and system changes, and some of them meant moving automations to a new tool or rebuilding them entirely. That's normal now, so I build things simply enough to rebuild quickly when a tool changes.

It's also why I don't think the old free tools are redundant. A local AI model needs a decent PC, and a paid AI subscription is a monthly commitment. Depending on your set-up and your budget, the free options still matter. What's genuinely new is that almost anything involving code or maths is now within reach of someone like me.

If you're starting today

This is what I'd tell a marketer starting now:

  1. Start with a problem, not a course. Pick something you actually want to fix or build.
  2. Start small. Get one thing working before you try to build anything clever.
  3. Don't aim for perfect. Get it working, with guardrails, then improve it.
  4. Write down every checkpoint. When something works, record what you did.
  5. Keep your old files. You'll want something to fall back on when a change breaks everything.
  6. Use the free stuff. I learned paid search itself from the free online certifications Microsoft and Google offered, collecting the badges. The same approach works for AI.

I didn't have a technical background, so what got me here was a reason to keep going, and getting it wrong a few times first.
