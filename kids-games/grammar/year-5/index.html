Year 5 Grammar and Punctuation

Year 5 is about adding extra information and making meaning clear: brackets, dashes and commas, relative clauses, and words that show how likely something is.

Below is everything on the Year 5 list in the national curriculum for England, in plain words with examples, plus the words your child will hear in class and a few easy ways to practise at home.

Punctuation in Year 5

Brackets, dashes or commas for extra information

This is called parenthesis. The sentence still makes sense if you take the extra bit out.

  • My aunt (who lives in Wales) is visiting.
  • My aunt – who lives in Wales – is visiting.

Commas to make meaning clear

A comma can change what a sentence means.

  • Let's eat, Grandma! (talking to Grandma)
  • Let's eat Grandma! (oh no)

Sentences and words in Year 5

Relative clauses

Add information about a noun with who, which, where, when, whose or that.

  • The boy who lives next door has a puppy.

How likely is it?

Adverbs like perhaps and surely, and modal verbs like might, should, will and must.

  • It might rain later.
  • You must wear a helmet.

Making verbs

-ate, -ise and -ify turn nouns or adjectives into verbs.

  • active → activate
  • real → realise
  • pure → purify

Verb prefixes

dis-, de-, mis-, over- and re- change a verb's meaning.

  • disagree
  • misbehave
  • overtake
  • recalculate

Linking paragraphs

Use words for time (later), place (nearby) and order (secondly) to link paragraphs together.

Year 5 builds on everything from the years before, so children keep using it all. See Year 4 for what came just before.

Words your child will hear in Year 5

The curriculum lists these terms for children to understand and use in Year 5.

modal verbA verb that shows how likely or needed something is: might, could, should, must, will.
relative pronounA word that starts a relative clause: who, which, that, whose, where, when.
relative clauseExtra information about a noun: the girl who won the race.
parenthesisExtra information dropped into a sentence with brackets, dashes or commas.
bracketThe ( ) marks around extra information.
dashThe – mark, used for extra information or a pause.
cohesionHow a piece of writing links up and holds together.
ambiguityWhen something could mean two different things.

Easy ways to practise at home

  • Take a plain sentence and add some extra information in brackets.
  • Play "definitely, probably, maybe" with tomorrow's weather.
  • Hunt for sentences in a book where a comma changes the meaning.
  • Play Punctuation Archery (Mixed challenge, to keep Year 1 to 4 punctuation sharp): pull back the bow and shoot the balloon that finishes the sentence. Get it wrong and someone throws a tomato at you.
