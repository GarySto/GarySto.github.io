The Speed of Change in Search Driven by AI

22 September 2026 · Updated 12 October 2026 · Gary Stow, performance marketing and AI

Written by Gary Stow with help from AI tools. Every fact is checked and every article is approved by Gary before it goes live.

Paid search has been around for twenty-odd years, and in all that time it has never stood still. Anyone who's worked in it for a while has lived through several reinventions already, so when people ask me whether AI is changing search too fast, my honest answer is that change is the one constant in this industry. What's different now is the speed.

A short history of the goalposts moving

When I started in digital marketing, the ad formats alone kept changing under us. Standard text ads gave way to expanded text ads, then dynamic search ads, then responsive search ads, and then Performance Max. Each change meant relearning how to write, test and measure, and each time the people who adapted early did best.

Running an account changed in the same way, with hands-on bid changes and hand-written ads gradually handed over to scripts, smart bidding and the platforms' own suggestions. I've told that part of the story in more detail in will AI replace PPC managers?

What's changing for advertisers right now

The changes I'm seeing most with advertisers at the moment are in two places.

The first is creative, where writing ad copy used to take hours of drafting variations by hand, and AI now produces strong first drafts in minutes, although they still need a person to judge whether they're right for the brand.

The second, and the one that surprises people, is reporting. Businesses want weekly and monthly reports, and they want to know what's happening in their market, which has always meant a lot of data pulling. Being able to get that data into the template and design a business wants, quickly, means reporting can be more accurate and more up to date, simply because it can be pulled far more often.

Search is moving into AI assistants

The bigger shift is where search itself happens. More research now happens inside AI assistants before anyone reaches a website, and ads are starting to appear inside those conversations too. That's a whole new area for paid search people to learn, with new placements, new ways people phrase what they want, and new reporting that has to be built.

It also changes how websites get found. On my own site I can see AI crawlers from several of the big companies visiting regularly, long before any of them cite it back, and I wrote up what that looks like in what Microsoft Clarity's AI Visibility actually shows you.

What doesn't change

For all of that, I still believe the role itself holds up better than people expect. Underneath it all, you're still paying for visits that need to turn into customers, and what moves is the toolkit and the know-how needed to use it.

So my advice is the same as it would have been at any point in the last twenty years. Keep a growth mindset, learn the new tools early, and stay on top of what's changing, because the people who do that will keep doing well whatever the next change turns out to be.
