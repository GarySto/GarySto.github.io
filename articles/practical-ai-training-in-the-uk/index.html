Practical AI Training in the UK

5 October 2026 · Updated 12 October 2026 · Gary Stow, performance marketing and AI

Written by Gary Stow with help from AI tools. Every fact is checked and every article is approved by Gary before it goes live.

Most people I talk to about AI don't need another overview of what it is. They need to sit down with the tools they actually have, on a problem they actually care about, and see what happens. That's what I mean by practical AI training: less watching, more doing.

I come at this from an unusual angle. Long before AI, I was teaching. I'm a fully qualified snowboard instructor, I spent years working in the Alps, and I hold a PTLLS qualification (Preparing to Teach in the Lifelong Learning Sector). More recently I've done one-to-one PPC mentoring, and I've spoken about AI and prompting at industry events. The lessons from a ski slope transfer surprisingly well to a room full of marketers.

What most AI training gets wrong

The problem with a lot of AI training is the same problem most training has. There's a set amount of content to get through, so there's no time to stop for the people who are struggling, or for the people who want to dig deeper. Anyone who needs a mentor, or needs to go at a different pace from the rest of the group, gets left behind or held back.

A lot of AI sessions also assume where people are. Some of the room has been using these tools daily for a year. Some have never opened one. Treating them as one group serves nobody. Ideally sessions are grouped by knowledge level, interest and intent, which is hard when every person in the room has their own goals.

Why one-to-one beats the lecture

I learned this one the hard way. When I first tried to learn Python, I did a couple of online courses. I got through "hello world", then got confused, lost and drifted off. It only started to click when I began building something real, with Claude alongside me, where I could see what I was doing and ask "why?" at every step.

That's the difference between a group course and an individual mentor. Good practical training should feel more like the second.

Start with "who is this for?"

Before I'd plan any session, I'd want to know who it's actually for, because the answer changes everything:

  • The business: then we're looking at real business problems and needs, and what a solution would be worth.
  • An individual: then the question is what they want to learn and what they want to walk away with.
  • A team: often the real goal is working better together, and AI is the vehicle for that.

Understanding what everyone wants, what they expect, and what a successful outcome looks like is the foundation of good training. Skip it and you end up delivering the content instead of helping the people.

I'd also want to know which AI tools the team has. Different tools, and different versions of the same tool, can do very different things, and whether people can use their own work data inside them changes the whole shape of a session.

What a practical session looks like

The shape I'd use:

  1. Prompt fundamentals. A good prompt structure first, because almost every disappointing AI result comes back to the prompt. This is the same material I presented at an IAB event, and it's the standard I use everywhere.
  2. The real problem. Run it as a lab or round table and ask what challenge the team actually wants AI to help with.
  3. Hands-on building. Small teams, working on that problem, with the tools they really have.
  4. Pitch back. Each team shows what it built, with a working demo if they got there, or just the idea if they didn't.

What I've seen work in the room

I was the AI expert at a full-day build event with around fifty people, split into teams. My job was to offer advice and experience, and to share where I think performance marketing automation is heading.

The order of that day worked: people demoed what they'd already built, then came the theory, then a hands-on workshop building solutions for the business. Seeing real builds first showed everyone what was possible before anyone sat through slides. Some of the ideas that came out of it were genuinely new to me.

There are two things I'd change: I'd give the hands-on part more time, because good ideas need it. And I'd always plan a follow-up, because the real test of a build day is whether any of it gets developed or adopted afterwards.

The biggest challenge is getting people started

I think one of the biggest challenges with AI is getting people to use it in the first place, and only then finding its limits. Training that ends with people having built something, however small, does more than any number of slides. If your team needs that kind of session, get in touch.
