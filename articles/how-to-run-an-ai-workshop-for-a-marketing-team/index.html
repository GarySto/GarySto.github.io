How to Run an AI Workshop for a Marketing Team

8 October 2026 · Gary Stow, performance marketing and AI

Written by Gary Stow with help from AI tools. Every fact is checked and every article is approved by Gary before it goes live.

If I had two hours with a marketing team tomorrow, I wouldn't spend them on slides. I think one of the biggest challenges with AI is getting people to use it in the first place, and only then finding its limits, so a good workshop gets hands on the tools as quickly as possible. This is how I'd run one, drawing on teaching snowboarding, one-to-one PPC mentoring, my PTLLS teaching qualification and the evenings I've spent teaching myself to build with AI.

The two-hour plan at a glance

Here's the shape of the session before I go through each part. The timings are what I'd aim for, and the hands-on block gets the biggest share on purpose because it's the part that always runs short.

| Time | Part | What happens | |, |, |, | | 0 to 15 minutes | Prompt fundamentals | Walk through the six-part prompt structure below | | 15 to 30 minutes | The real problem | Each team picks one challenge it genuinely wants help with | | 30 to 90 minutes | Hands-on building | Small teams build while I move between groups | | 90 to 110 minutes | Pitch back | Each team shows what it built, then we run the review checklist | | 110 to 120 minutes | Next steps | Agree what each team will try next and book the check-in |

Before the session, three questions

The planning matters more than the slides, and I'd want three answers before designing anything.

The first is who it's for. That answer changes the whole shape of the day.

The second is which AI tools they have. Different tools, and even different versions of the same tool, can do very different things, and whether people can use their own work data inside them changes what's possible in the room.

The third is where people are starting from. Some will have used these tools daily for a year and some will never have opened one. If I can, I'd group people by level, because in a mixed room the beginners get lost and the confident ones get bored.

Prompt fundamentals (15 minutes)

I open with the six-part prompt structure, because almost every disappointing AI result comes back to the prompt and this pays off for the rest of the session. The six parts are role, context, task, constraints, format and length, and I'd put the template below on the screen and ask everyone to copy it into a document they can reuse.

  • Role you're a [who the AI should act as]
  • Context here's the background it needs, [the situation, the audience, the data you're pasting in]
  • Task the one job you want done, [the thing you'd ask a colleague]
  • Constraints what to avoid or stick to, [tone, sources, things it must not do]
  • Format how the answer should look, [table, email, bullet summary]
  • Length how long it should be, [a number of words or a number of points]

I'd also warn people about the opposite mistake. A prompt that tries to control every detail ends up taking as long as doing the task yourself, and at that point you don't need AI for it. I go into the six parts in more depth in prompt engineering for marketers.

The real problem (15 minutes)

I run this as a lab or round table and ask the team which challenge they actually want AI to help with, because real problems keep people engaged in a way made-up examples never do. My own first build began like that. I wanted a trading dashboard, and once I'd got it working I asked myself whether I could turn it into an auto trading bot. Each step started with the same two questions, which were whether I could do it and what I'd need to learn to find out, and that's the habit I'd want each team to leave with.

It's also worth asking each team to say, in one sentence, what finished looks like. I once tried to build 12 automations to help manage my day, and the agent went off on a tangent and started building real technical infrastructure instead of the simple idea I'd asked for. I spent weeks of evenings on it before I realised I'd been following the system's lead rather than my original intent. A single agreed sentence at the start gives every team something to check back against.

Hands-on building (60 minutes)

Small teams work on their chosen problem with the tools they really have, while I move between groups to help anyone who's stuck, which is where the mentoring happens. Training often runs to a script, and a script has no slack for the person who's lost or the one who wants to dig further, so I'd rather give people the time they need than rush every team through the same walkthrough. I make the same point about group sizes and levels in practical AI training in the UK.

When someone is stuck, the first thing I check is usually the prompt. Most of the time the problem is whether it was written clearly enough and whether the AI was given enough context and data, or whether it had to guess. If a chat has gone on too long and is getting confused, I'd suggest starting a fresh one and pasting in a short summary of what's been decided so far.

Pitch back (20 minutes)

Each team shows what it built, with a working demo if they got there or just the idea if they didn't, and both count. After each pitch I'd run the same short checklist so the review feels routine rather than like an exam.

  • Did it solve the problem the team chose at the start, or a different one it drifted into
  • What did the AI get wrong, and how did you spot it
  • What did you give it that it would otherwise have had to guess
  • Who would check this output before anyone relied on it
  • What would you try next

Teaching the check matters more than any single prompt, because a team that leaves knowing how to question AI's output will keep improving long after the session. I explain why I'm so firm on this in the independent audit.

Handle the sceptics gently

Some people in the room will be sceptical, and that's fine. Forcing someone to use a tool they don't want to use isn't good for anyone, so I'd find out where their doubt comes from and aim for one small win on a challenge they genuinely have. I wrote more about that in how to get a sceptical colleague to actually try AI.
