Why change feels so hard, and how we can make it easier

25 November 2025 · Gary Stow, Senior Client Solutions Manager, Financial Services, Microsoft Advertising / Microsoft AI

Change. It's one of those words that can spark excitement for some and anxiety for others. In the workplace, it often leans toward the latter. Whether it's a new system, a shift in team structure, or a move back to the office, change disrupts the rhythm we've built. And humans love rhythm.

Why do we resist change?

At its core, resistance isn't about stubbornness. It's about survival. Our brains crave predictability because it gives us a sense of control. When that's taken away, uncertainty kicks in, and research shows uncertainty activates the same stress responses as physical pain. No wonder change feels uncomfortable.

There's also fear of the unknown. Will I succeed in this new environment? Will my role still matter? These questions aren't just logical, they're emotional. Add to that the identity factor: work isn't just tasks, it's part of who we are. When routines shift, it can feel like losing a piece of ourselves.

And then there's the status quo bias. We're wired to prefer what's familiar. It feels safe. So even when change is positive, our instinct is to cling to what we know.

The neurodiverse perspective

For those of us who are neurodivergent, like me, living with ADHD, change can hit even harder.

Why? Because many of us build personalised coping strategies to navigate the world. We create routines that help manage focus, sensory input, and energy. When those routines are disrupted, it's not just inconvenient, it can feel overwhelming.

Adjusting to new processes often means juggling ambiguity and rapid context-switching, two things that can drain cognitive resources fast. For someone with ADHD, that can lead to anxiety or burnout if support isn't there.

And here's the kicker: a lot of neurodiverse employees don't disclose their diagnosis. So if change programmes aren't inclusive by design, they risk leaving people behind. This isn't about ability, it's about environment. When workplaces fail to anticipate diverse needs, they unintentionally create barriers.

So, how do we navigate this?

If you're reading this as a leader, a teammate, or someone facing change yourself, a few things help.

Start with clarity. Ambiguity fuels anxiety, so explain the why behind the change and what it actually means for people day to day, not just the headline announcement. Give time and space too. Change isn't a light switch, and people need room to process and adapt at their own pace rather than being expected to arrive at acceptance on day one.

Offer flexibility where you can. Quiet spaces, remote options, assistive tech, these aren't perks, they're enablers, and treating them as optional extras misses the point of why they matter. And check in rather than checking out once the announcement's been made. A simple "how are you finding this?" can make someone feel seen in a way that a policy document never will.

If you're the one facing the change yourself, focus on what you can control. Build small routines within the new structure, even if they're modest, and don't be afraid to ask for what you need rather than assuming you should just cope.

Change will always be part of work. But how we handle it, for ourselves and for others, is what defines culture. For me, living with ADHD has taught me that change isn't just about systems, it's about people. And when we design with empathy, everyone wins.
