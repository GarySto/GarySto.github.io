Spotting Outliers in PPC Data: Real Insight or Just Noise

10 November 2026 · Gary Stow, performance marketing and AI

Written by Gary Stow with help from AI tools. Every fact is checked and every article is approved by Gary before it goes live.

An outlier in PPC data is a number that sits well outside what the rest of the account is doing, such as a sudden spike in spend, a keyword converting far worse than its neighbours, or a campaign with impressions and no clicks. Some outliers are the most useful insight in the account, and some are noise that people waste days chasing, so the skill is telling the two apart before you act.

Why outliers fool people

It happens all the time, usually because a generic term gets mixed in with the ones you meant to target, or because an issue at the search engine causes a spike that people then chase as if it were a trend. A big spike at least stands out, so it gets investigated. A gradual increase is much harder to catch, because no single day looks wrong, and that's the one that can run for weeks before anyone notices.

Real insight or just noise

The first thing I look at is impression share and what the account has achieved so far, and the second is whether one of our own changes explains it.

  • Explained by your own change. If impressions jumped but you also raised CPCs a lot, that's your answer.
  • Not explained. If CPCs and impression share are flat and spend suddenly spikes, that's worth investigating. It might even be worth contacting the search engine's account team.
  • Check the scale. Going from 1 to 10 is a huge percentage jump, and going from 100 to 110 isn't. A percentage on its own can make a tiny change look dramatic.

The Martin Lewis effect

The story I always come back to is from financial services, and I call it the Martin Lewis effect. Martin Lewis is a UK personality who makes recommendations on money and where people are missing out, from the best credit cards to take on holiday with the lowest fees, to insurance, pensions, mortgages and energy bills. When he comes on the telly and talks about one of those products, there's a real surge and spike in searches for it.

If you didn't know he'd been on, that spike would look like an outlier, or even like your own changes had suddenly worked. So when a spike isn't explained by anything you did, look at the trends before you act. Check Google Trends and your impressions, and see whether you can map the spike back to an event like that.

Knowing your own accounts is part of this. The same number can be normal in one account and a warning sign in another, and no tool knows that as well as the person who runs it.

The five steps I use to find the extremes

When I want to find the real extremes in an account, I work through the same five steps:

  1. Set two benchmarks: the account average and the account goals. Everything else gets judged against those.
  2. Find anything spending more than the goal with no conversion.
  3. Find anything converting at a CPA above the average, the target, or both.
  4. Find anything with no impressions, or with impressions but no clicks, meaning a CTR below the target or the average.
  5. Size how much of the account falls into each range. That tells you how much each fix is worth, so you know which one to do first.

The fifth step is the one people skip, and it's the one that decides where your time goes. A long list of keywords with no impressions can matter far less than a handful that are spending budget with no conversions.

Where AI helps

This kind of sorting is exactly what AI is good at, because it's large, repetitive and rule-based. Give it the two benchmarks and the four ranges, and it can sort an account much faster than you can by hand. It can also spot things you'd have missed, such as landing pages that convert far worse than the others while you were looking at keywords.

What it can't do is the judgement in the earlier section. It doesn't know you raised CPCs recently unless you tell it, so give it your change history along with the data, and treat anything it flags as a question to check, not an answer. The checking habits I use for that are in the independent audit: why I make AI check its own work.

What I'd do first

Write down your account average and your account goals before you open any data, then run the four ranges and size them. If one range is much bigger than the others, that's your first job. If a spike appears that none of your own changes explain, investigate it before you act on it.
