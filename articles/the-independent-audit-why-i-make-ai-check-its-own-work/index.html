The Independent Audit: Why I Make AI Check Its Own Work

8 October 2026 · Updated 12 October 2026 · Gary Stow, performance marketing and AI

Written by Gary Stow with help from AI tools. Every fact is checked and every article is approved by Gary before it goes live.

AI gets things wrong, and not just occasionally or only in obscure corners. It hallucinates, it gets distracted, it wanders off on tangents, it ignores what you asked for, and it overcomplicates simple things. If you use it seriously, you'll see all of those. So you need a habit that catches the mistakes before they cost you something.

Mine is what I call the independent audit.

What it is

At the end of any piece of work that matters, I ask the AI to go back over what it produced and check it: the facts and whether each one can be backed up, the accuracy of any numbers, whether it actually answers what I originally asked, and whether it followed the rules and constraints I gave it.

Sometimes I do that in the same chat. Sometimes I open a brand new chat, so the checker has no attachment to the answer it's checking. Either way, the point is the same: nothing gets acted on just because one pass of the AI said so.

The mistakes it's there to catch

These aren't hypothetical, and these are a few I've run into myself:

  • Numbers that drift. I've seen data rounded up or muddled until it was completely inaccurate, and it had to be revalidated and checked.
  • Mixing up the brief. During a banking deep dive it blurred two different customer types together. The numbers themselves were right, but the framing wasn't, and in hindsight my opening instruction hadn't pinned down which customers I meant.

That last one is worth dwelling on, because most of the time the problem isn't the model. It's whether I wrote the prompt clearly enough and gave it enough context, knowledge and data, or whether it had to guess. When it has to guess, it sidetracks itself.

Follow-ups need detail too

Something people miss is that your replies matter as much as your first prompt. A simple yes or no doesn't give the AI much to work with. It can't read your mind, and it can't see your facial expression when it's gone the wrong way. You have to give it more guidance than you would another person in the room.

The time it ran away from me

My worst example was the AI heading in the wrong direction altogether, when a handful of simple automations turned into weeks of evenings spent on a sprawling build I'd never asked for. I wrote the full story up in start small: the mistakes I made building AI tools, and a regular check against "is this still what I asked for?" would have caught it far sooner.

Same chat or new chat?

They both have their place, and staying in the same chat is great for keeping context going. But once a chat gets too long, you need to stop, collect what matters and start fresh. Long chats also burn through your tokens much faster.

I always recommend a knowledge file, a handover document, so one AI can pass the work on to the next. If a chat's gone badly, go back to a chat that was going well and ask it to write an even more detailed knowledge file, then carry on from there with a clean slate.

And I swear different chats have different personalities. You can go from an amazing chat to one that doesn't seem to know what 1+1 is. When that happens, the best thing I've found is to step away, go for a walk, and come back to a fresh chat with the knowledge file, rather than spending an hour arguing with one that's stopped listening.

Why it matters even more with marketing data

In PPC and analytics, a confident wrong answer is worse than no answer. If an AI tells you a keyword is wasting money, or a campaign has suddenly improved, you might act on it, with real budget. Checking the work is how you catch the times it's wrong before the money moves.

I've built the same idea into my own projects at home. The automation team that watches my demo trading account has departments that find issues and a supervisor whose job is to root-cause them before anything reaches me. My website's content team runs every draft through an audit against a facts file before I see it. Nothing gets acted on because one part of the system said so.

I read the work before it goes anywhere important, the same as I would with anyone's work.
