What Non-Technical Staff Should Learn First About AI

24 November 2026 · Gary Stow, performance marketing and AI

Written by Gary Stow with help from AI tools. Every fact is checked and every article is approved by Gary before it goes live.

AI training for non-technical staff works best when it starts with how to talk to the tool, what it can realistically do, and how it gets things wrong, before anyone learns a single feature. What I hope people take from a session is those three things, because they're more likely to help someone use AI safely and with confidence than a tour of every button.

The three things to learn first

  1. Prompt structure. How to talk to an AI and use it. Most disappointing answers come from a vague instruction, so this is the quickest win there is. Role, context, task, constraints, format and length is the structure I use, and I've shown how it works on a real job in an AI prompt framework for a PPC audit.
  2. Expectations. What AI can and can't do. A lot of what people hear is fear-mongering or morning news segments that set the wrong expectations. There are some amazing AI videos out there, but 99% of people won't have access to make that kind of groundbreaking technology, or the deepfakes that make the headlines, with the tools they actually use.
  3. Limitations and mistakes. AI makes mistakes, so it shouldn't be trusted blindly. People need to see what those mistakes look like, how to reduce them, and how to do better next time. Seeing a confident wrong answer for themselves, on a subject they know well, teaches that faster than any warning slide. Token limits belong here too, because AI isn't infinite. Every tool has a limit on how much it can read and write, and some burn through an allowance quickly, so it helps to know what you want before you start, keep each chat to one job, and only give it the files it actually needs.

The order matters too, because prompting first gives people an early success, expectations stop them being disappointed or alarmed, and the mistakes section makes sure the confidence they've just gained doesn't turn into blind trust.

What people are really afraid of

I don't know for sure, but my guess is change and the unknown. People like their comfort zone, and history and the media are full of examples of people fearing what they don't yet understand. AI arrives with both at once.

That's why I wouldn't open a session by telling people AI will change their jobs. Show them something small and useful first, on their own kind of work, and let the bigger conversation come later once they've used it.

How you'd know a month later that it worked

A session that felt good on the day can still change nothing, so I'd look for evidence a month on:

  • A questionnaire, or usage measurement and tracking, if the tools allow it.
  • People going from zero to one, meaning they're actually using it and spending tokens, not just saying they enjoyed the session.
  • Confidence rising, with people starting to push boundaries and build things of their own.

The second one is the clearest signal. A team that went from never opening the tool to using it every week has learned something, whatever they put on a feedback form.

Where I'd start

Run a short session on those three things using real examples from the team's own work, then check back in a month against the three signals. If usage hasn't moved, the problem is usually expectations or confidence rather than skill, and that's where to focus the next session.
