Practical AI Training in the UK

5 October 2026 · Updated 12 October 2026 · Gary Stow, performance marketing and AI

Written by Gary Stow with help from AI tools. Every fact is checked and every article is approved by Gary before it goes live.

Most people I talk to about AI don't need another overview of what it is. They need to sit down with the tools they actually have, on a problem they actually care about, and see what happens. That's what I mean by practical AI training: less watching, more doing.

I come at this from an unusual angle. Long before AI, I was teaching. I'm a fully qualified snowboard instructor, I spent years working in the Alps, and I hold a PTLLS qualification (Preparing to Teach in the Lifelong Learning Sector). More recently I've done one-to-one PPC mentoring, and I've spoken about AI and prompting at industry events. The lessons from a ski slope transfer surprisingly well to a room full of marketers.

What most AI training gets wrong

The problem with a lot of AI training is the same problem most training has. There's a set amount of content to get through, so there's no time to stop for the people who are struggling, or for the people who want to dig deeper. Anyone who needs a mentor, or needs to go at a different pace from the rest of the group, gets left behind or held back.

A lot of AI sessions also assume where people are. Some of the room has been using these tools daily for a year. Some have never opened one. Treating them as one group serves nobody. Ideally sessions are grouped by knowledge level, interest and intent, which is hard when every person in the room has their own goals.

Why one-to-one beats the lecture

My own route into coding proved this to me. A self-paced course lost me after the first lesson, and things only clicked once I was building something real with an AI helper I could question at every step, which I describe in how to learn AI as a marketer with no coding background.

Good practical training should feel closer to having a mentor beside you than sitting through a lecture.

Start with "who is this for?"

Before I'd plan any session, I'd want to know who it's actually for, because the answer changes everything:

  • The business: then we're looking at real business problems and needs, and what a solution would be worth.
  • An individual: then the question is what they want to learn and what they want to walk away with.
  • A team: often the real goal is working better together, and AI is the vehicle for that.

Understanding what everyone wants, what they expect, and what a successful outcome looks like is the foundation of good training. Skip it and you end up delivering the content instead of helping the people.

I'd also want to know which AI tools the team actually has access to, because that sets the limits of what we can do together in the room.

What a session looks like in practice

My sessions follow a simple arc: prompt basics first, then a real problem chosen by the team, then most of the time spent building in small groups, finishing with each group showing what it made. I've laid out the full agenda, and how I'd handle the sceptics in the room, in how to run an AI workshop for a marketing team, and what I saw at a fifty-person build day is in a Friday build day that showed how fast AI is moving.

The biggest challenge is getting people started

Most people only discover what AI can and can't do once they start using it, so training that ends with people having built something, however small, does more than any number of slides. If your team needs that kind of session, get in touch.
