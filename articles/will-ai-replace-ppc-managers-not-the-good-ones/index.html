Will AI Replace PPC Managers? Not the Good Ones

6 October 2026 · Updated 12 October 2026 · Gary Stow, performance marketing and AI

Written by Gary Stow with help from AI tools. Every fact is checked and every article is approved by Gary before it goes live.

I get asked this a lot, and my honest answer is no. I don't think the PPC manager's job changes as much as people fear. What changes is the tools, and what you need to know to use them well. To explain why, it helps to look back at how much of this job has already been automated, because AI is just the latest chapter of something paid search has been doing for twenty years.

What the early days actually looked like

When people say "a team of ten is now a team of two", they usually mean it loosely, but I mean something specific. In the early days of paid search, almost everything was done by hand: bids were adjusted manually, and every variation of ad copy was written by a person. Finding new keywords, spotting the search terms that needed adding as negatives, working out which ads were winning: all of it needed a human sitting in front of a spreadsheet.

Then the automation arrived in waves, first with rules and scripts, then bidding systems that took over the manual bid adjustments, and then the platforms started suggesting keywords and suggesting ad copy for you. Each wave took a chunk of manual work off the team, and each time the job changed shape and carried on.

Anyone who's worked in paid search for a while has seen the job reinvented more than once. Anyone who's been in it a while has lived through several of these shifts already. AI is a bigger one, but it's the same pattern.

What AI is genuinely good at now

The work AI does best in PPC is the work nobody wanted to do in the first place: going through thousands of rows looking for the handful that matter.

My clearest example is account audits, where a full audit used to take me around nine hours, because I was writing a 10,000-word document on multi-£m accounts. With an AI-assisted process, I get a complete first draft in about thirty minutes. I've written up exactly how that works, including what it got wrong, in how I cut a PPC audit from 9 hours to 30 minutes.

Reporting is changing just as quickly, and I've written about what I'm seeing there in the speed of change in search.

Where it still goes wrong

None of this means you can trust it blindly. I've watched AI get things wrong many times, and often very confidently.

Early on, I asked an AI tool about one ad platform and it confidently sent me help pages for a rival's product instead. I've also had it muddle figures badly enough that every number had to be checked again by hand. I've had it mix up two business verticals in the middle of a deep dive because my first prompt didn't make the vertical clear enough.

That's why the checking never goes away. Somebody has to know enough to look at the output and say "that's not right", and explain why.

The things that never get written down

This is the part of the job I don't think AI gets close to. A lot of what drives an account is never written down anywhere the system can see.

Clients often hold back performance or conversion data and never pass it back to the platform, which makes it very hard to optimise properly. Conversations happen at boardroom level about budget, strategy or people, and they don't always make it down to the PPC team doing the daily work. A plan can be in place one day and change in an instant the next, when it turns out the money isn't there.

I saw the most extreme version of that in travel when Covid hit. Every plan went out of the window in a second. Nobody knew when places would open again, or when holidays could be sold. But the business still needed to keep selling and bringing cash in, or it would have collapsed. The job became finding a way to keep doing that, without knowing when the product could actually be delivered. No bidding system or AI model was going to work that out, so people had to.

What I'd hire a junior for now

This is where I think the job really does change. If I hired a junior today, it wouldn't be for the copy-and-paste work, the work nobody wanted to do anyway. AI handles a lot of that now.

I'd want their fresh ideas, so I'd give them a problem or a challenge and ask how they'd solve it, or what their ideal world would look like. They haven't had years of experience or old systems skew their view yet, and that's valuable.

But they'd still need all the fundamentals. They need to be able to cover when I'm off, and they need to know enough to check what the system produces so it doesn't go rogue. You can't check what you don't understand.

What actually stays the same

The fundamentals of paid search haven't moved: we pay for clicks that lead to conversions, and the job is working out how to do that well for a particular business. A PPC manager is still responsible for that part of the business.

What changes is the toolkit, as ads are starting to appear inside AI assistants, which is a whole new area to learn, with new reporting skills that need building. That's new ground, but it's the same kind of new ground the industry has covered every few years since it started.

So, no, I don't think AI replaces good PPC managers. It takes over a lot of the copy-and-paste work. The managers who keep learning, stay on top of the tools and can spot a confident wrong recommendation will be fine.
