Getting a Website Ready for AI Search (llms.txt Template)

4 September 2026 · Gary Stow, performance marketing and AI

Written by Gary Stow with help from AI tools. Every fact is checked and every article is approved by Gary before it goes live.

I built this site to be a hub for what I'm learning in AI, and somewhere along the way I realised it has two different audiences. The first is people, and the second is machines, which means Google's crawler, Bing's crawler and whatever reads a page before an AI assistant decides to mention it. Getting found by the first group is normal SEO, whereas getting found and correctly cited by the second is a newer game. Most of it comes down to the boring plumbing under a site rather than the writing on top of it.

What follows is what I've changed on garystow.co.uk, in the order it mattered, with templates you can copy at the points where they help. If you'd like the free-tools angle on the same job, I've also written up getting a small website ready for AI search with free tools.

A sitemap and a robots.txt that agree with each other

Every crawler starts in the same two places, which are /sitemap.xml and /robots.txt. If robots.txt blocks something the sitemap lists, or the sitemap still contains pages you've removed or renamed, you're sending mixed signals before a single word of content gets read. Both files need to be short, accurate and updated whenever a new page goes live.

When I looked at a week of bot traffic on my own site, robots.txt was the most requested path of all, so the bots really do check it before anything else. A few minutes spent reading your two files side by side, asking whether every URL in the sitemap is one robots.txt allows, is some of the best value in the whole process.

IndexNow, so you don't wait around

Google and Bing eventually find new pages on their own schedule, and that can mean days or even weeks. IndexNow is a shared protocol that several search engines support, and it lets a site ping "this page changed, come and look now" the moment something is published. It's a small thing to wire in, but it's the difference between publishing an article and finding out about it in Search Console two weeks later.

A file written for AI assistants, not just search engines

There's a newer convention called llms.txt, which is a plain-language file at the root of a site that tells an AI assistant what the site is, who runs it and which pages matter most. It isn't a ranking signal that search engines use, but it's exactly the kind of concise, structured summary a language model can use when it's deciding whether and how to reference a site. Here's a template you can copy, save as llms.txt in the root of your site and fill in with your own details, swapping each web address placeholder for your own page addresses.

# Your Site Name

> One or two sentences on what the site is, who runs it and who it's for.

## Key pages

- [Home](your home page address) - what a visitor finds here
- [About](your about page address) - who you're and what you do
- [Best article](your best article address) - what it answers

## Articles

- [Article title](address of the article) - one line on what it covers
- [Article title](address of another article) - one line on what it covers

## Contact

- [Contact page](your contact page address)

Keep the descriptions factual and plain, because the point is for a model to understand your site in a few seconds. Update it when you publish something important, in the same way you'd update the sitemap.

Structured data, on every page that deserves it

Schema.org markup is the JSON-LD block you can't see but crawlers can, and it tells a machine unambiguously that this is a person, this is an article, and it was published on this date by this author. Search engines have used it for years to build rich results, and AI assistants can lean on the same structured signals to build confidence before they cite something. Every article on my site now carries Article schema, the homepage carries Person schema and the Work page carries schema for the project itself. Here's the Article version to adapt, which goes inside a script tag of type application/ld+json in the page head.

{
  
  "@type": "Article",
  "headline": "Your article title",
  "description": "One sentence summary of the article",
  "datePublished": "2026-01-31",
  "dateModified": "2026-01-31",
  "author": {
    "@type": "Person",
    "name": "Your Name",
    "url": "address of your about page"
  },
  "mainEntityOfPage": "address of this article"
}

Make sure every value in the markup matches what's visible on the page, because structured data that contradicts the content defeats the point of having it.

Watching what's actually crawling you

Clarity has an AI Visibility area that shows which bots are hitting your site and how often, broken down by operator and by purpose, so AI crawler traffic is separated from AI assistant traffic. Watching it told me the crawlers were showing up well before any of them were citing anything back, which is the honest middle stage of this whole process. You do the plumbing, then you wait, and then hopefully the citations follow. If you want to see how I read those numbers in more detail, my piece on understanding Clarity's AI visibility goes through it.

Right now the crawlers are visiting and citations are still at zero. That's expected for a young site and it isn't a failure, because what holds citations back at this stage is how much indexed content I have, not a technical fault.

What I'd tell someone starting from scratch

Do the sitemap and robots.txt first, because they're free and they take about ten minutes. Add structured data to your most important pages next, using the template above. Then add IndexNow and an llms.txt file once you're publishing regularly, since there's no point pinging search engines about a site that only has one page. None of this replaces good content. It simply makes sure the content you've already written gets found and understood correctly once it's out there.

If you want a next step to try today, open your robots.txt and sitemap in two browser tabs and check that they agree. Then paste the llms.txt template into a text file and fill it in for your three most important pages.
