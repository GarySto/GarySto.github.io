How to Run an AI Build Day That Actually Sticks

8 October 2026 · Updated 19 October 2026 · Gary Stow, performance marketing and AI

Written by Gary Stow with help from AI tools. Every fact is checked and every article is approved by Gary before it goes live.

The fastest way I know to get a team using AI is to stop talking about it and let them build something. A build day does exactly that: a group of people, the tools, a real problem and enough support in the room to get past the sticking points. Done well, it changes how people work. Done badly, it's an expensive day of slides. This is how I'd run one.

Start with real things people have built

Open with short demos of things people have actually made, not polished sales examples. It sets the tone straight away, because it shows everyone what's possible with the tools they already have before anyone sits through any theory. If the demos come from people in the room, even better, because it tells everyone else that people like them can do this.

Keep the theory short

A short burst of theory is enough: the structure of a good prompt, what the tools are good and bad at, and how to check the output. I cover the prompt basics I'd use in how to run an AI workshop for a marketing team. Anything longer eats into the part that matters.

Protect the hands-on time

The build is the bit that always runs out of time, so I'd protect it above everything else and make it the longest part of the day. Small teams work best, each with a real problem from their own work rather than a made-up exercise. A real problem keeps people invested, and whatever they build has a chance of being used the following week.

Put experienced builders in the room

The most useful thing you can give a team is someone who has been stuck in the same places before. I taught myself to build with AI, mostly alone in the evenings, and I know exactly where people get stuck because I got stuck there too. Someone who can spot that early and nudge a team past it is the difference between a team that finishes with something working and a team that spends the afternoon going in circles.

Finish with show and tell

End with every team showing what they made, however rough. People leave having built something rather than having heard about it, and the ideas from other teams spark more ideas. It's often where the most surprising builds appear, ideas nobody planned for at the start of the day.

Check back a few weeks later

A build day creates a lot of energy, and the real test is what happens to it afterwards. I'd book a check-in a few weeks later to see which ideas were developed further, which were adopted and which stalled, and why. That follow-up is where most build days fall short, and it's the easiest part to fix.

Why now

A team can now get a working prototype in a single afternoon that would have taken a lone beginner weeks not long ago, as long as the right support is in the room. That says a lot about how quickly the tools, and people's skills with them, are improving, and it's the best argument I know for getting teams hands-on sooner rather than later.
