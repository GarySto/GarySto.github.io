Thriving in tech with a neurodiverse brain

23 October 2025 · Gary Stow, Senior Client Solutions Manager, Financial Services, Microsoft Advertising / Microsoft AI

Let's be honest, working with me isn't always easy. I live with ADHD and a sprinkle of autism. That means I can be hyperactive, intensely focused one moment, and distracted the next. Emotional swings happen. For new managers or teammates, this can feel like a rollercoaster.

Why I'm writing this

I've debated sharing this for a while. Not because I'm ashamed, far from it, but because being open about neurodiversity in the workplace can feel risky. Will people judge? Will they see me as difficult or unpredictable?

But here's the truth: my ADHD is part of who I am. It shapes how I think, how I work, and how I show up every day. And while that sometimes makes life challenging, for me and for those around me, it's also the reason I've been able to deliver some of my proudest achievements in tech.

If you're neurodiverse, or if you manage someone who is, I hope this gives you insight, reassurance, and maybe even a little inspiration.

The reality of my brain

My mind races, jumping from idea to idea like a browser with fifty tabs open. When I'm in the zone, I can hyperfocus for hours, forgetting lunch and everything else. But then there are days when distractions creep in, or emotions swing unexpectedly.

For new managers or teammates, this can be challenging. And for me, it's exhausting trying to mask those traits to fit a mould that was never designed for brains like mine.

The flip side: my superpowers

Here's the part I love: those same traits are also my superpowers. Creativity flows when I connect dots others don't see. Hyperfocus allows me to dive deep into complex problems and deliver solutions under pressure. Resilience comes from navigating a world that often feels like it wasn't built for me.

One example: during a recent challenge to build dashboards and insights, we faced a last-minute data issue that could have derailed all the hard work. My brain went into overdrive, fuelled by caffeine and a fair amount of adrenaline. I mapped out a workaround in hours, pulling insights from multiple systems, reaching out to connections, and delivering a solution that kept the wheels turning. That ability to think laterally under pressure is my ADHD at work.

Finding the right environment

Early in my career, I tried to hide my differences. I worked late to compensate for distractions, and I didn't even know I had ADHD, I was diagnosed late, so management was unable to support me.

Things changed when I joined teams that valued inclusion, not just as a buzzword, but as a practice. Managers who asked what I needed to do my best work instead of assuming one-size-fits-all. Colleagues who understood that my energy wasn't chaos, it was passion. Simple adjustments made a huge difference: clear priorities instead of vague goals, flexibility to work in bursts rather than rigid hours, and permission to use tools like noise-cancelling headphones and visual task boards.

What managers can do

If you manage someone who's neurodiverse, start with curiosity. Ask, don't assume. Focus on outcomes rather than rigid processes. And most importantly, celebrate strengths. Neurodiverse employees often bring creativity, problem-solving, and resilience, qualities every team needs.

Advice for my neurodiverse peers

Own your story. You don't have to share everything, but being open, when it's safe to be, can build trust and understanding. Experiment with tools and techniques until you find what works for you. And remember, the right environment exists. Seek out cultures that value flexibility and inclusion, not just in words, but in actions.

Why this matters

Tech thrives on diversity, not just of gender or ethnicity, but of thought. Neurodiverse minds bring perspectives that drive innovation. When we create spaces where those minds can thrive, everyone wins.

I'm proud of what I've achieved, not despite my ADHD and autism, but because of them. They've shaped my resilience, my creativity, and my ability to see the world differently.
