Prompt Engineering for Marketers: The Six Parts I Teach

31 October 2026 · Gary Stow, performance marketing and AI

Written by Gary Stow with help from AI tools. Every fact is checked and every article is approved by Gary before it goes live.

Prompt engineering for marketers is the skill of getting what is in your head into the instruction you give an AI, so the model doesn't have to guess what you meant. I teach it as six parts, in the same order every time, because once the structure is habit you stop having the long back and forth that most people think is just how AI works.

The six parts of a good prompt

This is the six-part structure I've presented to rooms of marketers, and the one I still use myself:

  1. Role. Tell the model who it is for this job, for example "you are a senior account strategist". It sets the level and the point of view of the answer.
  2. Context. Everything the model can't see: the client, the goal, what has already happened, what the numbers are for.
  3. Task. The actual thing you want done, in one or two plain sentences.
  4. Constraints. What it must stick to and what it must avoid.
  5. Format. How you want it back, such as a table, bullet points, a short email or a slide outline.
  6. Length. How long the answer should be.

Using that structure gets you the answer you wanted much quicker, and it saves the back and forth. That back and forth is really the model trying to find the information you didn't give it in the first place.

Why the same data can give you completely different answers

A useful way to see this is to take one spreadsheet you know well and ask about it three ways. Ask for a summary, and you get a summary of the numbers. Ask what might be causing the changes, and you get a discussion of causation and correlation. Ask what to do next, and you get a list of things to investigate and where the opportunities might be. Nothing about the data changes between those answers, only the instruction does, and that's the whole argument for taking the prompt seriously.

People also tend to ask the same two things whenever AI comes up, which are how and what's next. They hear big claims about what AI can do and want the steps, the prompt and the method. The six parts are the most useful answer I can give to the first of those, because they turn "use AI for your reporting" into something you can actually type.

The most common mistake is missing context

When I look at prompts that go wrong, the cause is almost always the same, because people don't give the model enough context. We assume it knows what we mean. It can make a decent attempt without the extra information, but the answer is never as good as it would have been with it.

The fix is to write down the things you would explain to a new colleague on their first day. Who the client is, what good looks like for them, and what you already know. If you would have to tell a person, you have to tell the AI.

Two ways a prompt goes wrong

There are two failure modes, and they sit at opposite ends.

  • The over-long prompt that micromanages. If you want a 300-word document, say so, let the model do its thing, and correct the style afterwards if it isn't right. If you're going to get to the level of dictating every sentence in advance, you might as well have just written it yourself, because at that point you don't need AI.
  • The very light prompt. With hardly any context, the AI is free to bring in anything. That is where most of what people call slop or hallucinations comes from, and it comes down to the quality of the prompt.

The six parts sit in the middle of those two. Enough to point the model in the right direction, without writing the answer for it.

My own lesson with header images

I learned this on my own website. My early prompts for article header images weren't specific enough, so the pictures all came out looking much the same and didn't match what I had in mind. I hadn't told the model what I didn't like, why I didn't like it, or that I wanted variety across the set. Once I went back and changed the prompts to say those things, the results changed with them.

Every AI needs a slightly different approach

I treat each AI I use as a different personality, and adjust the same structure for each one:

  • A local model on my own PC needs very specific, less friendly prompts, because it has a smaller memory to work with.
  • My personal Copilot needs me to be more forceful, or it asks a lot of questions before it gives me an answer.
  • Claude can work across many files and projects at once, but it uses up my allowance quickly, so I need to know what I want before I start.

The six parts stay the same for all three, and what changes is how much detail goes into each one and how firmly it's worded.

What I'd tell a marketer starting today

Write your next prompt with all six parts, even if it feels like overkill for a small job, and compare the answer with what you would normally have got. Most people only need to see that difference once.
