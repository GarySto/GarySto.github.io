Explaining AI to Non-Technical People: The Graduate Test

8 October 2026 · Gary Stow, performance marketing and AI

Written by Gary Stow with help from AI tools. Every fact is checked and every article is approved by Gary before it goes live.

When I talk about AI to people who are not technical, I usually compare it to a graduate fresh out of university. They have loads of great ideas and suggestions, they have read everything and they work fast, but they have no practical, real world experience. You have that experience, and your job is to use it to guide their ideas towards your problems.

Why the Comparison Works

Most explanations of AI go wrong in one of two directions. Either they make it sound like magic that will do everything, or they make it sound like a gimmick. The graduate comparison sits in the honest middle, and it gives people an instinct for how to work with AI without needing to understand how it works underneath.

People already know how to get good work out of a bright graduate. You give them context, because they do not know your clients or your history. You set clear rules about what good looks like. You check their work before it goes anywhere important, and you expect the odd confident answer that turns out to be wrong. Every one of those habits is exactly what makes AI useful.

It Explains the Mistakes Too

The comparison also prepares people for the moments AI gets it wrong. I have seen AI outputs make things up with complete confidence, ignore instructions, or make bold claims nobody can back up. A graduate can do that too, not out of bad intent but because they do not yet know what they do not know. That is why I ask AI for an independent audit at the end of important work, the same way you would read a graduate's report before it goes to a client.

It Puts the Human in Charge

The most useful thing the comparison does is put the person back in charge. A lot of nervousness about AI comes from feeling replaced. Thinking of it as a very capable graduate who still needs your judgement turns that around, because your experience is the part it cannot copy. You understand the human element, the things that never get said or written down, and when something just does not feel right.

Get Them Using It

Explaining AI only gets people so far. I personally think one of the biggest challenges with AI is getting people to use it first, and only then finding the limitations. So the best explanation is usually a short one followed by hands-on time, where people try it on a problem they actually have and see for themselves where the graduate shines and where it needs guiding.

Keep It Simple

I often feel like the idiot in the room when people start using big words, and I prefer the direct, simple approach. When you are explaining AI to a non-technical audience, simple is not dumbing down, it is the whole point. If they leave knowing how to brief it, check it and guide it, they will use it.
