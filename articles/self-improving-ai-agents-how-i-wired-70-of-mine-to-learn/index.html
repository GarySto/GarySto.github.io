Self-Improving AI Agents: How I Wired 70+ of Mine to Learn From Their Results

7 November 2026 · Gary Stow, performance marketing and AI

Written by Gary Stow with help from AI tools. Every fact is checked and every article is approved by Gary before it goes live.

I have more than 70 AI agents running at home now, spread across a website team, a trading team on a practice account, a job-hunting team and an outreach team. You can see them all as lights on my agent map, and as little people walking around the village. For months I thought of them as a self-improving system, because they write notes for themselves and pick up where they left off. On Saturday 7th November 2026 I sat down to check whether that was really true, and for a lot of the system it was not.

This piece is what I found, what I changed, and the simple test I now use on every agent. If you run any kind of automation in a marketing team, from bid rules to AI writing tools, the same test applies.

What I mean by a self-improving AI agent

I do not mean an AI model that retrains itself. None of my agents change their own model, and none of them should. What I mean is simpler and more useful: an agent whose results come back to whoever made the decision, so the next decision is better than the last.

The articles on this topic call it a closed loop. An open loop goes suggest, act and then nothing comes back. A closed loop goes suggest, act, measure, and then feed what happened into the next suggestion. Every agent that only makes suggestions is open loop by default, because nobody built the road back.

The four-question test

Before the audit I wrote down four questions to ask of every team, and I would ask them of any automation you own.

Does everything it produces have a reader, or is it writing reports into a folder nobody opens?

Does everything it needs have a supplier, or is it guessing because the data never arrives?

When it makes a change, does anyone check later whether the change helped?

Does that result go back to the agent that made the decision, so it can learn from it?

The first two questions are about wiring, and the last two are about learning. Most of what I found failed the last two.

What the audit found

I mapped every report my teams write against every script that runs, which took a script of its own because there are more than 500 of them. Then I went team by team. These are the real findings, not examples.

My Content Upgrader, the agent that rewrites my weakest articles, had thrown away 48 rewrites in six days. It kept retrying the same six articles up to three times a day, and the reasons were nearly always the same two: a sentence reused wording from one of my other articles, or it linked to an outside source that no research had checked. Another agent had already spotted the loop and rested those articles, but resting them only delays the next failure, because nothing fixed the cause.

My outreach team had emailed 24 newsletters, communities and schools, and not one replied or linked. Worse, every time it went looking for new targets it had no idea that nothing had worked, so it would have carried on doing the same thing. Nine of those 24 emails went to schools about my free learning games, which is not what I want to be known for at work.

The results log was empty, even though my goals charter says success is measured by real outcomes, such as interviews, recruiter messages, enquiries, consulting leads and side income, all kept in one file. That file had been empty since 23rd October, and nothing wrote to it or read it. Results did exist, but in three separate places that never met.

My trading team's supervisor writes ideas for new rules into a list so they can be tested with no money. Nothing ever read that list, so the ideas were never tested. The agent that proposes trading changes could see which proposals I had approved, but never whether they had helped. And my website's "did it work?" checker had said "no per-page data" for about a month, while per-page numbers for 53 pages sat in another file it was never given.

What I changed

None of the fixes needed a cleverer model, because they all needed a road back.

One results log. A new script pulls replies, links earned, job applications and the weekly trading results into one file every day. It brought in 18 results on its first run. A simple page lets me log the things the system cannot see, such as a recruiter message, in about ten seconds. Every supervisor now starts its weekly thinking from that file, and the weekly summary leads with it.

Tell each team its own hit rate. The outreach agent is now told its real reply rate by type of target and by page pitched before it picks new targets. Zero out of 24 is uncomfortable to read, but it is the most useful number that team has ever been given.

A did-it-work desk. Every trading change I approve is now compared on real closed trades before and after, once at least ten trades have closed, and it always shows the trade count so a small sample is never mistaken for proof. On its first run it grouped 51 approvals into 14 decisions, and it showed my second trading model got worse after a series of data fixes, which backed up my earlier call to pause it.

A separate tester for ideas. When the supervisor proposes a new rule, a different worker replays it on years of real prices against the current rule, costs included, and sends the verdict back.

Fix the cause, not the symptom. The Upgrader now cuts the one copied or unsourced sentence and checks the draft again, instead of binning the whole rewrite. It also asks my research agent for sourced facts before it rewrites an article that cites outside sources.

A daily connections check. My system auditor already asked "did every job run?". It now also asks "does every report have a reader, and are the learning loops still closing?". On its first run it found the Upgrader loop by itself.

Testing a rule before trusting it: the 5% stop

My practice trading game sells any trade that falls 5% below the buy price. That rule felt sensible, so I had never measured it, until the audit made me ask how often it sells a trade that would have gone on to win.

I replayed 99 of the game's picks from my research lab on real five-minute prices. The 5% rule sold 15 trades that went on to win, and turned £50 into £47.24. A 15% limit kept those winners and turned the same £50 into £150.63. These stocks move 5% as normal noise. That is practice money and a small sample, not advice, but I would never have known without the loop, and the rule had been running for a month.

The rules I keep

Self-improving does not mean self-approving. A few rules keep this safe, and they are the ones I would give any team doing the same.

Don't check your own work, get someone else to check. It is the same idea as the independent audit I use on any AI work. Every supervisor move is checked by a separate worker, and I added a weekly Improvement Coach that reads how every team is doing and suggests at most three improvements.

Every suggestion comes with the why. The coach has to quote its evidence, name the result it should move and say how we will know in four weeks whether it worked. Then it is judged against that.

I sign off anything involving money, my name or the trading rules. The system is meant to be the brain, and I only step in for the fundamentals.

Small samples are a reason to keep testing, never a reason to go live.

A prompt you can copy to audit your own automations

You can run the four-question test with any AI assistant in a few minutes. Write a short list of the automations your team runs, such as bid rules, scheduled reports, AI copy tools or alerts, then paste this prompt above it:

You are auditing a marketing team's automations for missing feedback loops.
For EACH automation listed below, answer four questions:
Question 1: What does it produce, and who actually reads or uses that output?
Question 2: What data does it need, and where does that data come from?
Question 3: When it changes something, who checks later whether the change helped, and how?
Question 4: Does that result get back to the person or rule that made the decision?
Mark any answer that is "nobody", "nothing" or "not sure" as a GAP.
Then give me a table with these columns: automation, output reader, data source,
follow-up check, result goes back to, gaps.
Finish with the ONE gap that touches the most money, and a simple before-and-after
check for it: what to measure, over how many days, and what result would mean
the change helped. Do not invent details I have not given you; ask me instead.

My automations:

The last line matters most, because asking the AI to name what it does not know stops it filling the gaps with guesses, which is the same reason my agents' work is always checked by a separate worker.

To keep the results somewhere, a spreadsheet with six columns is enough: date, automation, what changed, what you expected, what happened after four weeks, and keep or undo. Mine is a CSV file that every supervisor reads, but a shared sheet does the same job for a team.

How to apply this in a marketing team

You do not need 70 agents to use the test. Take any automation you already run, such as automated bid rules, AI-written ad copy or a weekly report, and ask the four questions. Most automated bid rules change something every day, and very few teams check four weeks later whether each change made money. Most AI writing tools never learn which of their drafts the team rejected and why.

Start with the one automation that touches the most money, add a before-and-after check, and make sure the result reaches whoever owns the rule. That is the whole idea of a self-improving system. It is less about clever AI and more about plumbing, and plumbing is something any marketer can build.
