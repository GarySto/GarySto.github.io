How to Automate PPC Reporting with AI and Still Trust the Numbers

7 November 2026 · Gary Stow, performance marketing and AI

Written by Gary Stow with help from AI tools. Every fact is checked and every article is approved by Gary before it goes live.

Automating PPC reporting with AI means letting the AI pull the data, build the tables and charts and draft the commentary, while a person decides what the report is for and checks every number before it goes anywhere. Most of a weekly report can be handed over now, and the part you keep is the part that makes the report worth reading.

What a PPC report has always had to answer

Whether I was client side or agency side, there was always a request to look back over the last 7, 14 or 30 days. What was the performance, what were the impressions and the click-through rate, how much did we spend, and what was our CPA and our ROI?

The better requests went further and asked what we missed. Did the market move without us catching it, could we have done better, how could we learn from it, and what do we need to learn going forward? Those questions are where a report earns its keep, because they turn a set of numbers into a decision.

Tests made reporting even more important, because the data had to prove the change before it was rolled out. When responsive search ads replaced expanded text ads as the default, I needed to be happy with the type of data I was seeing before running any of it. When I first implemented Performance Max, or tried any of the betas and alphas the search engines put me on, I always ran a test, which meant regular reports from those A/B tests so I could see how things were doing.

What a good PPC report includes

A good report covers three things, and the third matters most:

  • The market and where the opportunities are. Not just your own numbers, but what's happening around them.
  • Anything skewing the data. Spikes and surges in traffic, with the reason behind each one.
  • Whatever senior leadership is actually asking for. There's no point in a report nobody reads, however much work went into it.

Outside events are a big part of the second point, and they can come from anywhere. In financial services, anything Martin Lewis talks about can drive a surge in searches. During a recent eclipse, searches for kitchen colanders rose because someone on TV said they were a good way to view it. A report that shows the spike without the reason leaves the reader guessing.

The parts I'd hand to AI today

I'm confident most of a report can be handed to AI. That covers pulling the data, building the tables and graphs, and adding context and reasons to the numbers.

The context can come from your emails, Teams transcripts and call notes, as long as it's written down somewhere the AI can read. If the reason for a change only lives in your head, write it down. You never want a single point of failure in a team, and an undocumented reason is exactly that, for the AI and for the colleague who covers your holiday.

What stays with a person is deciding what the report is for, and signing off what it says. The AI can explain a spike, but someone who knows the client has to confirm the explanation is right before senior leadership reads it and acts on it.

Why the numbers need checking

AI rounding or mixing up numbers is something I've only recently discovered, and it changed how I treat every figure in an AI-written report. My checks are simple and I run them in this order:

  1. Ask the AI for an independent audit of its own work, looking specifically at the numbers.
  2. Ask for citations, meaning the exact rows or formulas it used for each figure.
  3. Check it myself. Open the spreadsheet, use a calculator, and click the links.

None of that takes long once it's a habit, and it's far quicker than explaining a wrong number to a client. The phrase I keep coming back to is "hold your LLM accountable". I wrote up the first of those checks in more detail in the independent audit: why I make AI check its own work.

Where I'd start

Pick the one report you produce most often and split it into the data, the tables and charts, the commentary and the sign-off. Hand the AI the first three, give it your notes for the commentary, and keep the sign-off. Run the three checks on every number for the first few weeks, and you'll quickly see how far you can trust it with your own data.
