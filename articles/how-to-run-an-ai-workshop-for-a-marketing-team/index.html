How to Run an AI Workshop for a Marketing Team

8 October 2026 · Gary Stow, performance marketing and AI

If I had two hours with a marketing team tomorrow, I would not spend them on slides. I personally think one of the biggest challenges with AI is simply getting people to use it in the first place, and only then finding its limitations. A good workshop gets hands on the tools as fast as possible.

Before the Session: Find Out Which AI They Have

The first thing I would want to know is which AI the team is actually using. Different AI tools, and even different versions of the same tool, can do very different things, and whether people have access to their own work data inside the tool changes everything. That one answer would completely change the structure of the session, so I would ask it before planning anything else.

The Two-Hour Agenda

This is roughly how I would shape the time:

  • Prompt fundamentals. Start with the basics of a good prompt structure, because almost every disappointing AI result I see comes back to the prompt.
  • The real problem. Run it as a lab or a round table and ask the team which challenge they actually want AI to help solve. Real problems keep people engaged in a way that made-up examples never do.
  • Hands-on building. Split into small teams and get them working on that problem with the tools they really have, rather than watching a demo.
  • Pitch back. Each team pitches its idea to the room, with a working demo if they got that far, or just the idea if they did not. Both count.

Explain AI in Plain Words

Not everyone in a marketing team is technical, and nobody needs to be. The way I explain AI is that it is like a graduate fresh out of university, with loads of great ideas and suggestions but no real world experience. The team has that experience, and their job is to use it to guide the AI towards their actual problems.

Do Not Force It

Some people in the room will be sceptical, and that is fine. Forcing someone to use a tool they do not want to use is not good for anyone. It is better to understand where the scepticism comes from and show them one small, simple win on a challenge they genuinely have. Baby steps break down barriers far better than a hard sell.

After the Workshop

When I joined a hands-on build day recently, the teams had a good amount of time, but it still felt too short for the ideas they had. So if I were running it, I would protect the hands-on part above everything else. The part I would most want to add is a follow-up. It is one thing to build something in a room, and another to see whether the ideas get developed further or adopted by the business, so I would always plan a check-in to see what stuck.
