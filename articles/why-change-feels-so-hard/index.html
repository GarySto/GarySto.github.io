Why change feels so hard, and how we can make it easier

25 November 2025 · Updated 12 October 2026 · Gary Stow, performance marketing and AI

Written by Gary Stow with help from AI tools. Every fact is checked and every article is approved by Gary before it goes live.

Change can spark excitement for some people and anxiety for others, and at work it often leans towards anxiety. Whether it's a new system, a new team structure or a move back to the office, change disrupts the rhythm we've built, and most of us rely on that rhythm more than we realise.

The biggest change I've worked through

The most extreme change I've lived through at work was in travel during the pandemic. Almost overnight the whole industry lost the thing it sold, with borders shut and no date for reopening, yet the company still had to find ways to take bookings and keep money coming in.

There was no rulebook for that, so we found a way to do the job with what we had and kept adjusting as the rules moved.

Why we resist change

Resistance is rarely just stubbornness, because our brains like predictability because it gives us a sense of control, and when that goes, uncertainty kicks in, along with worries about whether our role will still matter that are emotional as much as logical.

There's also the pull of the familiar. It shows up with new technology too, because I have family members who accept that AI could help them and still won't go near it. Even when a change is positive, the instinct is to hold on to what we know.

The neurodiverse side

I have ADHD, and change can hit people like me harder. Many of us build our own routines and systems to manage focus and energy, and mine include scheduled reminder emails every morning, midday and afternoon that put my work in priority order. When a change knocks those routines out, it isn't a small inconvenience, it can be genuinely overwhelming.

Adjusting to a new process often means coping with ambiguity and switching context quickly, two things that drain energy fast. And many neurodiverse people don't tell their employer, so if a change programme isn't inclusive by design, it can leave people behind without anyone noticing.

What helps

If you're leading a change, a few things make a real difference:

  • Explain the why. Say what the change actually means for people day to day, not just the headline.
  • Give it time. People need room to adapt at their own pace rather than being expected to accept it on day one.
  • Offer flexibility. Quiet spaces, remote options and assistive tech are enablers, not perks.
  • Check in afterwards. A simple "how are you finding this?" can make someone feel seen in a way a policy document never will.

If you're the one facing the change, focus on what you can control. Rebuild small routines inside the new structure, and ask for what you need rather than assuming you should just cope.

Change and AI

A lot of what I write about is AI, and getting people to adopt it is really a change problem rather than a technology one. Small steps, a clear reason and time to adjust are what get a sceptical team using a new tool.
