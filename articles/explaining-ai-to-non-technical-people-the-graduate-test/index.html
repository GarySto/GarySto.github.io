Explaining AI to Non-Technical People: The Graduate Test

8 October 2026 · Updated 12 October 2026 · Gary Stow, performance marketing and AI

Written by Gary Stow with help from AI tools. Every fact is checked and every article is approved by Gary before it goes live.

When I talk about AI to people who aren't technical, I usually compare it to a graduate fresh out of university. They have loads of great ideas and suggestions, they've read everything and they work fast, but they have no practical, real-world experience. You have that experience, and your job is to use it to guide their ideas towards your problems.

Where I use it

I use this comparison all the time at work, whenever someone asks me about the AI tools we have and how they should think about them. It's relatable, because almost everyone has worked with a bright new starter, and it gives people an instinct for how to work with AI without needing to understand how it works underneath.

Why it works

Most explanations of AI go wrong in one of two directions, either making it sound like magic that will do everything or like a gimmick. The graduate comparison sits in the honest middle.

People already know how to get good work out of a bright graduate. You give them context, because they don't know your clients or your history. You set clear rules about what good looks like. You check their work before it goes anywhere important, and you expect the odd confident answer that turns out to be wrong. Every one of those habits is exactly what makes AI useful.

It explains the mistakes too

The comparison also prepares people for the moments AI gets it wrong. Ask it about one product and it might happily answer about a competitor's instead, and I've had it hand me figures that turned out to be wrong. A graduate can make the same kind of mistake, not out of bad intent but because they don't yet know what they don't know, and that's why I always check important AI work before it goes anywhere, the same way you'd read a graduate's report before it goes to a client.

Where the comparison breaks down

It isn't perfect, and the main risk is getting too personal. If the comparison starts to sound like a dig at actual graduates, or at someone in the room who's new to their job, people can get offended. I keep it about the AI's lack of experience, never about anyone's ability.

It puts the human in charge

The most useful thing the comparison does is put the person back in charge. A lot of nervousness about AI comes from feeling replaced, and thinking of it as a very capable graduate who still needs your judgement turns that around, because your experience is the part it can't copy. You understand the human side, the things that never get said or written down, and when something just doesn't feel right.

Then get them using it

Explaining AI only gets people so far, because the real breakthrough comes once they start using it. The best explanation is usually a short one followed by hands-on time, where people try it on a problem they actually have and see for themselves where the graduate shines and where it needs guiding.
