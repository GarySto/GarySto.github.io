Practical AI Training in the UK and How I'd Plan a Session

5 October 2026 · Gary Stow, performance marketing and AI

Written by Gary Stow with help from AI tools. Every fact is checked and every article is approved by Gary before it goes live.

Picture a snowboard lesson where half the group has never strapped in and the other half is already bored on the nursery slope. A good instructor does not teach that group the same way, and the same is true of an AI session in a room full of marketers. Most people I talk to about AI do not need another overview of what it is. They need to sit down with the tools they actually have, on a problem they actually care about, and see what happens, which is what I mean by practical AI training.

I come at this from an unusual angle, because long before AI I was teaching. I am a fully qualified snowboard instructor, I spent years working in the Alps, and I hold a PTLLS qualification (Preparing to Teach in the Lifelong Learning Sector). More recently I have done one-to-one PPC mentoring and spoken about AI and prompting at industry events, and the lessons from a ski slope transfer surprisingly well to a meeting room.

What most AI training gets wrong

The problem with a lot of AI training is the same problem most training has. There is a set amount of content to get through, so there is no time to stop for the people who are struggling or for the people who want to dig deeper. Anyone who needs a mentor, or needs to go at a different pace from the rest of the group, ends up either left behind or held back.

A lot of sessions also assume where people are. Some of the room has been using these tools daily for a year while others have never opened one, and treating them as a single group serves nobody. Ideally sessions are grouped by knowledge level, interest and intent, which is hard when every person in the room has their own goals.

Why one-to-one beats the lecture

My own route into coding proved this to me. I tried a couple of Python courses, one on Microsoft Learn and a free one I think came from Coursera, and I got as far as printing "hello world" before I was confused and lost. It only clicked when I started working with Claude, set up GitHub and began a trading project, because I could see what I was building and ask questions at every step. I describe that in more detail in how to learn AI as a marketer with no coding background, and it is the reason I think good practical training should feel closer to having a mentor beside you than sitting through a lecture.

Start with who the session is for

Before I plan anything I want to know who the session is for, because the answer changes everything. If it is for the business, we look at real business problems and needs and what a solution would be worth. If it is for an individual, the question becomes what they want to learn and what they want to walk away with. If it is for a team, the real goal is often working better together, with AI as the vehicle for that.

Understanding what everyone expects and what a successful outcome looks like is the foundation of good training, and without it you end up delivering content instead of helping people. I also want to know which AI tools the team has access to, because that sets the limits of what we can build together in the room.

Five questions a manager can ask before booking anything

If you manage a team and want to know where they really are with AI, these are the five questions I would put to them before choosing any training. You can ask them in a short round table or send them round in a simple form.

  • Which AI tool do you have access to at work, and which one do you actually open in a typical week?
  • What is one repetitive task in your week that eats time and that you would happily hand to an assistant?
  • If you asked an AI for help today, could you describe the role, context, task, constraints, format and length you would give it?
  • What worries you most about using AI on your work, whether that is mistakes, data or being seen to rely on it?
  • When an AI gives you an answer, how do you currently check that it is right before you pass it on?

The answers tell you very quickly whether you need a first-steps session for people who have never started, a prompting session for people who use it badly, or a deeper build session for the people already running ahead. They also show you who is nervous and why, which matters more than most people expect.

What a session looks like in practice

I would start with the fundamentals of a prompt, because that single skill saves the most time. The structure I teach has six parts, which are a role, the context, the task, any constraints, the format you want and the length, and using it gets you the answer you want much sooner and saves the back and forth that is really the model hunting for what you meant. The most common mistake is not giving enough context, so the thing in your head never makes it into the prompt, and the opposite mistake is a prompt so long and controlling that you might as well have written the thing yourself. I go through the six parts in prompt engineering for marketers.

My early prompts for website header images were vague, so every image came out samey and nothing matched what I had in mind, because I had never said what I disliked, why, or that I wanted variety. In the end I went back and changed the prompts.

After the basics I would run the session as a lab day or a round table, depending on the team. The team chooses a real challenge they want AI to help with, and most of the time is spent building in small groups, with a pitch at the end where each group shows what it made, either as a working demo or as an idea. The point is to get people using the tool, because I think the biggest barrier to AI is getting people to use it at all, and only then do they find its limits. I lay out the full agenda and how I would handle the sceptics in how to run an AI workshop for a marketing team.

Three things I want everyone to leave with are a prompt structure they can reuse, realistic expectations of what AI can and cannot do, and a feel for what its mistakes look like. Much of what people see on morning television sets the wrong expectations, so seeing real mistakes up close is part of the training, and I cover the checking habit I teach alongside it in the independent audit.

A one-page session discovery sheet

Here is the sheet I would fill in with a client before planning a session, and you are welcome to copy it as it is.

  • Who the session is for, whether that is the business, an individual or a team.
  • What a successful outcome looks like for each of those people a month from now.
  • Which AI tools the group can use, and which of them they use today.
  • The rough split of the room between never used it, use it sometimes and use it daily.
  • One real problem or repetitive task that each person or group wants to solve.
  • The main worries in the room, such as accuracy, data, change or looking foolish.
  • How we will measure success afterwards, with either a short questionnaire or tracking of usage.

A session has worked if you can see it in behaviour a month on. That can be a short questionnaire or simple usage tracking, and the clearest signs are people going from zero to one by actually using the tool, or confidence rising and people pushing boundaries and building their own things.

The biggest challenge is getting people started

Most people only discover what AI can and cannot do once they start using it, so training that ends with people having built something, however small, does more than any number of slides. If someone really does not want to try it, I do not think you can force them, but you can understand their scepticism and look for a simple problem where a small first step helps.
