How to Get a Sceptical Colleague to Actually Try AI

7 October 2026 · Updated 12 October 2026 · Gary Stow, performance marketing and AI

Written by Gary Stow with help from AI tools. Every fact is checked and every article is approved by Gary before it goes live.

If someone really doesn't want to try something, there's nothing you can do until they're ready, and forcing it isn't good for anyone. That's the honest starting point. But in my experience, most people who say they're against AI aren't against it at all. They're worried about something, and they haven't yet seen it solve a problem they care about.

The biggest sceptic I've won over

The biggest sceptic I've won over is my wife. She was very anti-AI. A lot of that came from what she was hearing on TV about AI taking jobs, and she was genuinely worried about what it would mean for her own job and her industry. No amount of me telling her what it could do was going to change that.

What changed it was our house: we were redesigning a bedroom, and I showed her that she could describe her vision to Copilot and it would generate an image of the room. Suddenly she was looking at the thing in her head, on a screen, in seconds. That definitely won her over, and it had nothing to do with work.

The lesson I took from it: you don't win sceptics with a demo of what AI can do for someone else. You win them when it solves something that matters to them.

The ones I haven't won over

I haven't won everyone over, and some older relatives of mine still won't have it. Even when they agree with what it can do and see the benefits, their default is to refuse it, and to want everyone back in the office using old-school technology, the way they had to.

I've stopped pushing, because pushing harder just hardens the resistance. The door stays open, and if a moment comes where it solves a problem they actually have, it'll land then.

Explain it as help, not a threat

A lot of the fear is about replacement, so the way you describe AI matters. I explain it as an assistant, a junior member of staff, or a volunteer: someone willing to do the time-consuming, laborious tasks you don't want to do.

I still love pulling together a PowerPoint. But copying and pasting line after line, hour after hour, is where AI is fantastic. Think back to the jobs that used to go to whoever was newest in the team, the work that was just Ctrl+C and Ctrl+V. That's where AI earns its keep. Some people are paid for copy and paste, but most of us are paid for our brains and what we bring to the business. Framed that way, people tend to see it as help with the parts of the job they never liked.

Find one small, real problem

The best way in is to look at the challenges that person already has and ask whether one of them has a simple AI fix. Not a grand transformation, one small thing:

  • an email they dread writing
  • a report that eats an afternoon every week
  • meeting notes that never get written up
  • a pile of data they never have time to look through

One win on their own work does more than any demo. Then take it in baby steps. Each small success makes the next one easier.

Be honest about the limits

AI can be confidently wrong, and I've had it answer questions about one ad platform with links and answers from a completely different one. I've seen it round numbers until they were simply inaccurate. Telling a sceptic that up front, and showing them how you check the work, builds far more trust than pretending it's perfect. I wrote about the checking habit in the independent audit.

It's really about change

Getting someone to try AI is a change management problem more than a technology problem. People need to understand why, what it means for them day to day, and they need time. I wrote more about that in why change feels so hard, and how we can make it easier, and about running sessions that get a whole team started in practical AI training in the UK.
