Why I Built Free Learning Games My Daughters Can Play Together

2 November 2026 · Gary Stow, performance marketing and AI

Written by Gary Stow with help from AI tools. Every fact is checked and every article is approved by Gary before it goes live.

My daughters' favourite thing on this whole website isn't anything I've written about AI. It's the Where's Gary game in my AI village, where they hunt for a little cartoon version of me among the houses, and they kept asking for more. So I built them free learning games they can play together, even though one of them is three and the other is eight.

It also came from something that had been bugging me for a long time. I'd watch the girls on their iPads trying to play the "free" learning games, and they'd constantly get interrupted by adverts, usually for something with no relevance to a child their age, and sometimes for something no child should be shown at all. They'd get frustrated, the learning would stop, and I knew I could do better than that for them.

Why it's hard to find games for a three-year-old and an eight-year-old

One of my girls is three and learning her colours, the other is eight and racing through her times tables. Most games suit one of them and bore the other, and there are surprisingly few games for a three-year-old that are simple enough for her to follow along without a grown-up doing it for her.

I also wanted to help my eldest with her spellings and maths in a way that didn't feel like more homework. So I gave myself a simple brief: games they can play side by side, each at her own level, with the learning built in quietly.

What I built

There are four Kids Games, all free to play in a web browser, and each one has a bit of learning hiding inside it.

Seek & Find is the one they play together. The older player reads clues and works out times tables, division and halves to discover what she's looking for, while the younger one hears every picture read out loud and counts to three. Neither of them can finish without the other, and at the end they go looking for Daddy, who is hiding somewhere in the picture.

Times Table Pegs is for my eldest. Every right answer earns a ball to bounce off glowing pegs, a bit like the classic Peggle games, across five levels. It covers every times table from 1 × 1 to 12 × 12 plus the matching division, the trickier facts turn up more often as the levels go on, and anything she gets wrong comes back a little later.

Paint Splash is for my youngest, and her big sister can join in. You run through paint buckets to paint the floor, then stand in the bubble bath to wipe it all clean. Every so often a gentle voice asks her to find a colour, so she's picking up colour names, shapes and cause and effect without anyone telling her to.

Spell & Build is the newest. You read a simple clue, work out the word and spell it, and every word builds part of a picture, like a rocket, a castle or a cake. It has the national curriculum spelling lists from Year 1 to Year 6, or you can type in this week's words from school, and there's a spelling test mode that reads 15 or 20 words aloud and marks them at the end.

Every child should see themselves in the game

The characters started off looking like my girls, which is lovely for us and no good for anyone else's children. So there's a Make it yours panel where any family can change the names, boy or girl, skin tone, hair, eyes and glasses, and do the same for the grown-ups, so their children see themselves on the screen.

Both children always get exactly the same outfits to choose from, because nobody wants that argument at bedtime. Everything a family types stays on that device. The names are never sent to me or anyone else, and there's no account to create.

The learning they don't notice

I'm not a teacher, so I've stuck to what schools already use. The times tables run from 1 × 1 to 12 × 12, which the national curriculum expects children in England to know by the end of Year 4, and the spellings are the statutory word lists from the national curriculum spelling appendix. I've also put those spelling lists on their own pages, so you can print them for the fridge.

For my youngest it's colours, shapes and counting, with everything spoken out loud. Plenty of schools already use apps for quick-fire times tables practice, and this is simply another way to keep those facts fresh at home, in something that feels like a game rather than a test.

Built in the evenings, with AI

I'm self-taught when it comes to code, and I built all of this in the evenings with Claude, Anthropic's AI, for two very demanding customers. I describe what I want in plain English, it writes the code, and then I test it, change it and test it again.

It hasn't all been smooth. The first version worked perfectly on my laptop, but on the girls' older iPads the pictures came up blank. Their version of Safari was missing one drawing feature the games relied on (rounded corners, of all things), so I worked around it, and now I check everything on an old Windows 10 laptop, my Windows 11 PC and both of the girls' iPads running iOS 15 before it goes live.

What it won't do

These games won't replace school, and they won't replace sitting down and reading a book together. They haven't been reviewed by teachers, and an iPad is still a screen, so I treat them like any other screen time.

What I can promise is that every game has a natural place to stop. Nobody loses and there are no timers, which helps a lot when it's time for bed.

Why it's free

There are no adverts, no accounts and nothing to buy, and the Kids Games pages don't use analytics or cookies either. That was the whole point. All of the code is free on GitHub for other parents and teachers, and if you work in a school there's a one-page summary for teachers you can print or share.

If you've got an idea for a game you'd love to see, get in touch. Depending on how complex it is, I may be able to build it for free.
