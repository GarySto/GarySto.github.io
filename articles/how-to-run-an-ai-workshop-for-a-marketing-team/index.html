How to Run an AI Workshop for a Marketing Team

8 October 2026 · Updated 12 October 2026 · Gary Stow, performance marketing and AI

Written by Gary Stow with help from AI tools. Every fact is checked and every article is approved by Gary before it goes live.

If I had two hours with a marketing team tomorrow, I wouldn't spend them on slides. I think one of the biggest challenges with AI is getting people to use it in the first place, and only then finding its limits, so a good workshop gets hands on the tools as quickly as possible. This is how I'd run one, drawing on teaching snowboarding, one-to-one PPC mentoring, my PTLLS teaching qualification and being the AI expert at a fifty-person build day.

Before the session: three questions

The planning matters more than the slides, and I'd want three answers before designing anything.

Who is it for? If it's for the business, we're working on business problems. If it's for individuals, it's about what each person wants to learn. If it's for a team, the real goal is often working better together. That answer changes the whole shape of the day.

Which AI tools do they have? Different tools, and even different versions of the same tool, can do very different things, and whether people can use their own work data inside them changes what's possible in the room.

Where are people starting from? Some will have used these tools daily for a year and some will never have opened one. If I can, I'd group people by level, because a mixed room means the beginners get lost and the confident ones get bored.

The two-hour agenda

  1. Prompt fundamentals. I start with the structure of a good prompt, the same material I presented at an IAB event and use everywhere. Almost every disappointing AI result comes back to the prompt, so this pays off for the rest of the session.
  2. The real problem. I run it as a lab or round table and ask the team which challenge they actually want AI to help with, because real problems keep people engaged in a way made-up examples never do.
  3. Hands-on building. Small teams work on that problem with the tools they really have, while I move between groups to help people who are stuck, which is where the mentoring happens.
  4. Pitch back. Each team shows what it built, with a working demo if they got there or just the idea if they didn't, and both count.

Leave room for different paces

Training often runs to a script, and a script has no slack for the person who's lost or the one who wants to dig further. In the hands-on part, I'd rather give people the time they need than rush every team through the same walkthrough.

Handle the sceptics gently

Some people in the room will be sceptical, and that's fine. Forcing someone to use a tool they don't want to use isn't good for anyone, so I'd find out where their doubt comes from and aim for one small win on a challenge they genuinely have. I wrote more about that in how to get a sceptical colleague to actually try AI.

Teach the check, not just the build

Every team should leave knowing how to check what AI gives them. I'd build a quick review into the pitch-back: what did the AI get wrong, and how did you spot it? That habit matters more than any single prompt, and I explain why in the independent audit.

After the workshop

At the build day I helped at, the hands-on part was the bit that ran out of time. So if I were running it, I'd protect that part above everything else, and I'd book a check-in a few weeks later to see which ideas actually stuck, because that's the real measure of a workshop.
